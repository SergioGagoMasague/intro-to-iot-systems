2  Development Boards and the IoT Kit

TipLearning objectives

  1. Use voltage, current and Ohm’s law to choose a current-limiting resistor for an LED.
  2. Build simple circuits on a solderless breadboard and explain how its strips are connected.
  3. Explain what a microcontroller is and why it replaced fixed-function circuits in most products.
  4. Identify the parts of a development board and what each one does.
  5. Describe the ESP32 and the DevKit V1 board used in the kit, including the pins you must avoid.

2.1 Why it matters

Every IoT device starts as a circuit on a bench. Before you can write code that senses or acts, you need to know how electricity reaches a component, how much of it the component can take, and how a microcontroller board fits into the circuit. Most “my code doesn’t work” problems in a first IoT course turn out to be circuit problems: an LED wired backwards, a missing ground, a component on the wrong breadboard row.

This chapter builds the smallest useful circuit, makes it blink, and then explains the development board that will run all the code in the rest of the book.

2.2 Concepts

Voltage, current and resistance

Three quantities describe what happens in a simple circuit:

  • Voltage (V, measured in volts) is the electrical “pressure” between two points. It is what pushes charge around a circuit. A USB port supplies 5 V; the ESP32’s pins work at 3.3 V.
  • Current (I, measured in amperes or milliamperes, mA) is the rate at which charge flows. Components are damaged by too much current, not by voltage alone.
  • Resistance (R, measured in ohms, Ω) opposes current.

They are related by Ohm’s law:

\[ V = I \times R \]

Current only flows around a closed loop: from the supply, through the components, and back to ground (GND), the reference point that all voltages are measured from. If any part of the loop is open, nothing happens.

Lighting an LED safely

A light-emitting diode (LED) conducts in one direction only. Its longer leg, the anode, must face the positive supply; the shorter leg, the cathode, faces ground. Once it conducts, an LED holds a roughly fixed voltage across itself, called its forward voltage (\(V_f\)): about 2.0 V for a red LED, and around 3 V for blue or white ones.

An LED has almost no resistance of its own once it conducts. Connect it straight across a supply and the current rises until something burns out. A current-limiting resistor in series sets the current:

\[ R = \frac{V_{supply} - V_f}{I_{LED}} \]

For a red LED driven from a 3.3 V ESP32 pin, aiming for 10 mA:

\[ R = \frac{3.3\ \text{V} - 2.0\ \text{V}}{0.010\ \text{A}} = 130\ \Omega \]

Resistors come in standard values, and it is always safe to round up. The kit uses 220 Ω, which gives about 6 mA: plenty for an indicator LED, and gentle on the microcontroller pin.

Breadboards

A solderless breadboard lets you build and change circuits without soldering. Inside, metal clips connect groups of holes:

  • In the main area, each row of five holes is connected. The rows on either side of the central gap are not connected to each other. Chips and modules straddle this gap so that each pin gets its own row.
  • The long power rails along the edges run the length of the board. By convention, the red rail carries the supply voltage and the blue rail carries ground. On some boards the rails are split in the middle, so check before relying on them.

The kit uses two 830-point breadboards side by side, with the ESP32 bridging the seam. The rails carry 3.3 V and GND, except for one short rail section reserved for the servo’s 5 V supply (Lab 1).

From fixed circuits to microcontrollers

Suppose you want the LED to blink once per second. One classic solution is a 555 timer: a chip whose output switches on and off at a rate set by a resistor and a capacitor. It works, but changing the behavior means changing components. Making it blink twice and pause, or blink faster when a button is pressed, means designing a new circuit.

A microcontroller replaces the fixed circuit with software. It is a small computer on a single chip, with a processor, memory and input/output pins. The same chip can blink an LED, read a sensor or run a network stack, depending on the program you load:

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);
  delay(1000);
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);
  delay(1000);
}

Microcontrollers are cheap (often well under a dollar for simple ones), so it is usually cheaper to use one than to design a custom circuit. This is a large part of why connected devices became affordable.

What a development board adds

A bare microcontroller chip is hard to use on a bench: its pins are tiny, it needs a stable power supply, and loading a program onto it requires special hardware. A development board wraps the chip with everything needed to experiment:

  • A power circuit: a voltage regulator that turns 5 V from USB into the 3.3 V the chip needs.
  • A programming interface: usually a USB-to-UART bridge chip (Chapter 5), so a laptop can upload programs and exchange text with the board.
  • Basic I/O: at least one LED and a reset button, often a second “boot” button.
  • Header pins: the chip’s I/O pins brought out at breadboard-friendly spacing.

Arduino and the ESP family

The Arduino project made microcontroller development accessible to people with no electronics background. It combined an inexpensive open-hardware board, the Arduino Uno based on an 8-bit AVR microcontroller, with a simple programming environment and a large library ecosystem. Its programming model (a setup() function and a loop() function, Chapter 3) is still the most common entry point to embedded programming.

The original Uno has no network connection. Adding Wi-Fi meant stacking an expansion board, called a shield, which added cost and size. The ESP8266 and later the ESP32, both from Espressif Systems, changed this by putting a capable processor and a Wi-Fi radio on the same inexpensive chip. A chip that combines a microcontroller with radios and other advanced peripherals is often called a system on a chip (SoC).

The ESP32 can still be programmed with the Arduino framework, so the simple programming model carries over unchanged.

The ESP32 DevKit V1

The kit uses the ESP32 DevKit V1, a widely cloned board built around the ESP32-WROOM-32 module. Its parts are:

  • The ESP32-WROOM-32 module (the metal can), which contains the ESP32 chip, its flash memory, a crystal and a printed antenna. Keep the antenna end clear of metal and wires.
  • A USB-to-UART bridge (typically a CP2102 or CH340 chip) for uploading and for the Serial Monitor. If your laptop does not recognize the board, the bridge’s driver is the usual culprit.
  • A 3.3 V regulator fed from USB or from the VIN pin.
  • Two buttons: EN resets the chip; BOOT (GPIO 0) puts it into upload mode if automatic upload fails.
  • A power LED and, on most versions, a blue LED on GPIO 2.

Not every pin is equally safe to use. These rules prevent most beginner problems:

Each pin can safely source or sink only a few tens of milliamperes. Drive LEDs through resistors, and never power motors directly from a GPIO pin.

2.3 In depth (optional)

Optional, in-depth explanation. This section goes beyond the rest of the chapter into the math and physics behind it. Nothing later in the book depends on it; skip it on a first reading.

Currents, resistors and heat on the kit

Sizing an LED resistor. A lit LED holds a roughly constant forward voltage \(V_f\) across itself. The resistor takes the rest of the supply, so by Ohm’s law

\[ I = \frac{V_\text{GPIO} - V_f}{R}. \]

With a 3.3 V pin, a red LED (\(V_f \approx 2.0\) V) and 220 Ω, \(I = 1.3 / 220 \approx 5.9\) mA: bright enough to see, well within a pin’s limits. A green or blue LED (\(V_f\) from about 2.2 to 3.0 V) draws less, and appears dimmer, with the same resistor. The resistor dissipates \(P = I^2 R \approx 7.7\) mW, far below its ¼ W rating.

Without the resistor, the only thing limiting current is the pin driver’s own output resistance (tens of ohms) and the LED’s small dynamic resistance: the current can exceed the pin’s rating, and the pin or the LED can be damaged. The simulator in Activity 1 does not model this.

Pin limits add up. The ESP32 lets you set each pin’s drive strength; at the default setting a pin sources roughly 20 mA, and the absolute maximum is around 40 mA. What matters as much is the total drawn through the chip’s power pins. Use pins to signal, not to power things: anything over a few milliamperes deserves a transistor.

Pull-up resistors. With INPUT_PULLUP, an internal resistor of roughly 45 kΩ connects the pin to 3.3 V. When the button closes, the current is \(3.3 / 45\,000 \approx 73\ \mu\)A: negligible for an LED-sized budget, but not for a coin-cell device that holds a button down for hours.

The regulator gets warm. The DevKit’s linear regulator drops USB’s 5 V to 3.3 V and turns the difference into heat:

\[ P_\text{reg} = (V_\text{in} - V_\text{out})\, I_\text{load}. \]

During Wi-Fi bursts of 250 mA, \(P = 1.7 \times 0.25 \approx 0.43\) W. A small regulator package rises in temperature by its thermal resistance \(\theta_{JA}\) times that power; at a typical 90 °C/W, about 38 °C above ambient. It works, but leaves little margin, which is one reason not to hang extra loads on 3V3.

Exercise. You want an LED current of 10 mA from a 3.3 V pin with a green LED of \(V_f = 2.2\) V. Which standard resistor value would you choose, and what current do you actually get?

2.4 Design trade-offs

Choice Consider
Development board vs. custom board Development boards are ideal for prototypes and small runs. Products use a custom circuit board with only the parts they need, which lowers cost, size and power.
Module vs. bare chip A pre-certified radio module (like the WROOM-32) avoids designing and certifying an antenna circuit yourself.
Arduino framework vs. vendor SDK (ESP-IDF) Arduino is faster to learn and has many libraries. The vendor SDK gives full control of power modes, the RTOS and security features.
5 V vs. 3.3 V parts Mixing them needs level shifting. Prefer 3.3 V parts with a 3.3 V microcontroller.

2.5 Common pitfalls

  • LED backwards. It simply stays dark. Flip it.
  • No resistor. The LED may glow briefly and fail, and the GPIO pin can be damaged.
  • Component in the wrong row. Both legs of a resistor in the same five-hole row connect it to nothing.
  • Split power rails. Half the board has no power.
  • Charge-only USB cables. The board powers up but the computer never sees it. Use a data cable.
  • Strapping pin held at the wrong level. The board refuses to upload or boot. Disconnect the component on that pin and try again.
NoteAdvanced track

Reading a datasheet. Find the ESP32-WROOM-32 datasheet and locate: the absolute maximum voltage on any I/O pin; the recommended operating voltage range; the current drawn during Wi-Fi transmission; and the current in deep sleep. Compare the transmit and deep-sleep figures. By what factor do they differ, and what does that imply for a battery-powered design?

Beyond the ESP32. Newer Espressif chips use RISC-V cores (ESP32-C3, C6, H2) and add radios such as 802.15.4 for Thread and Zigbee. Other families popular in IoT include Nordic’s nRF52 and nRF54 (Bluetooth Low Energy), Silicon Labs’ EFR32 (multi-protocol) and the Raspberry Pi RP2040/RP2350 (no radio, but very low cost). Compare one of them with the ESP32 on power, radios and security features.
