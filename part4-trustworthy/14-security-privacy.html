14  Security and Privacy

TipLearning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain why security and privacy failures in IoT have physical and personal consequences.
  2. Describe the challenges that make IoT harder to secure than traditional IT.
  3. State the security requirements of an IoT system, from confidentiality to forward and backward secrecy.
  4. Threat-model a device with STRIDE.
  5. Describe attacks and countermeasures in the cloud, fog and sensing domains.
  6. Use the basic cryptographic building blocks (hashes, MACs, symmetric encryption, signatures, TLS) correctly, and protect messages against tampering and replay.

14.1 Why it matters

When a web service is breached, data leaks. When an IoT system is breached, the damage can be physical. Researchers have shown that cars can be controlled remotely through their entertainment systems, and that implanted medical devices can be reprogrammed wirelessly. Doctors disabled the wireless features of a pacemaker implanted in a former US vice president because of exactly that concern. Botnets built from insecure cameras and routers have taken down major internet services (Chapter 9).

IoT also raises privacy concerns that traditional computing rarely did. Smart-home sensors, wearables and cameras record intimate details of daily life: when people are home, when they sleep, how they move, even their heartbeats. Facial, speech and activity recognition amplify what raw sensor data can reveal, and combining metadata from several devices can expose far more than any one of them. UCI’s TIPPERS project, an IoT testbed for privacy-preserving smart buildings, studied exactly these risks.

Security and privacy are not features to add at the end. They shape every layer of the design.

14.2 Concepts

Why IoT is hard to secure

Security requirements

Following Dabbagh and Rayes (Dabbagh and Rayes 2017), a secure IoT system must provide:

  • Confidentiality: only the intended parties can understand exchanged messages.
  • Integrity: messages cannot be altered without detection.
  • Availability: the service keeps working. Denial-of-service attacks target this.
  • Authentication: every party is who it claims to be: devices, users, servers.
  • Authorization: every party may perform only the operations it is permitted to.
  • Freshness: data is current. Replay attacks resend an old, valid message, such as “unlock”, to drive a device back into an old state.
  • Non-repudiation: a party cannot deny an action it performed.
  • Forward secrecy (in the group-membership sense): a device that leaves the network cannot understand communications exchanged after it leaves.
  • Backward secrecy: a device that joins cannot understand communications exchanged before it joined.

Threat modeling with STRIDE

Before choosing defenses, identify what can go wrong. Threat modeling systematically asks how each part of a system could be attacked. Microsoft’s STRIDE mnemonic names six threat categories, each violating one security property:

A simple process: draw the system’s data flows (devices, gateways, brokers, cloud services, apps), mark the trust boundaries where data crosses from one party’s control to another’s, and for each element and flow ask which STRIDE threats apply. Then rank threats by likelihood and impact, and choose mitigations for the important ones.

A three-domain view of IoT security

It helps to divide an IoT system into three domains, each with its own attacks. The taxonomy below follows Dabbagh and Rayes (Dabbagh and Rayes 2017):

  • the cloud domain, which performs heavy computation and storage;
  • the fog domain (the edge), which aggregates, preprocesses and stores data close to the devices (Chapter 18);
  • the sensing domain, the smart objects themselves and their networks.

Cloud domain

Cloud IoT applications run in virtual machines (VMs) or containers that share physical servers, managed by a hypervisor. Sharing creates attack opportunities:

Fog domain

Fog devices virtualize computing resources close to the objects, so they inherit all cloud-domain attacks, and add some of their own:

  • Authentication and trust. Fog devices may be owned by many, less well-known parties, and a mobile device may hop between them. Devices must authenticate the fog node and decide whether to trust its owner; reputation systems can help.
  • Migration over the internet. VMs that follow mobile users move between fog nodes over the public internet, not a private data-center network, so migrations must be encrypted and authenticated.
  • Denial of service. Lower computing capacity makes fog nodes easier to overwhelm.
  • Privacy. A fog node can infer the location and habits of every connected device. Even wireless signals alone can reveal people’s presence, location and breathing; signal obfuscation can make such inference harder.
  • Containers. Lightweight containers share the operating system kernel as well as the hardware, which creates more opportunities for leakage and hijacking than full virtualization.

Cryptographic building blocks

You do not need to design cryptography, and you never should. You do need to pick the right building block for each job:

Three rules prevent most mistakes:

  1. Use standard, well-reviewed libraries (mbedTLS on the ESP32) and standard protocols (TLS) rather than inventing your own.
  2. Encryption is not integrity. An encrypted message can still be modified in ways that change its meaning unless it is also authenticated. Use AEAD or add a MAC.
  3. Integrity is not freshness. A correctly signed “unlock” message can be replayed. Include a counter, a timestamp or a nonce, and reject messages that are old or repeated.

Signing messages on the kit

The ESP32 includes mbedTLS with hardware-accelerated SHA and AES. The following sketch attaches an HMAC and a monotonic counter to each command, so the receiver can detect both tampering and replay:

#include <mbedtls/md.h>

// Shared secret: provisioned per device, never hard-coded in a real product
static const uint8_t KEY[32] = { /* 32 random bytes */ };

String hmacHex(const String& msg) {
  uint8_t mac[32];
  const mbedtls_md_info_t* sha256 = mbedtls_md_info_from_type(MBEDTLS_MD_SHA256);
  mbedtls_md_hmac(sha256, KEY, sizeof KEY,
                  (const uint8_t*)msg.c_str(), msg.length(), mac);
  char hex[65];
  for (int i = 0; i < 32; i++) sprintf(hex + 2 * i, "%02x", mac[i]);
  return String(hex);
}

// Sender: "counter|command|mac"
String signCommand(uint32_t counter, const String& cmd) {
  String body = String(counter) + "|" + cmd;
  return body + "|" + hmacHex(body);
}

// Receiver: accept only authentic messages with a counter it has not seen
bool verifyCommand(const String& msg, String& cmdOut) {
  static uint32_t lastCounter = 0;              // persist this in flash in practice
  int a = msg.indexOf('|'), b = msg.lastIndexOf('|');
  if (a < 0 || b <= a) return false;
  String body = msg.substring(0, b);
  String mac  = msg.substring(b + 1);
  if (hmacHex(body) != mac) return false;       // tampered or wrong key
  uint32_t counter = msg.substring(0, a).toInt();
  if (counter <= lastCounter) return false;     // replayed or out of date
  lastCounter = counter;
  cmdOut = msg.substring(a + 1, b);
  return true;
}

In production code, compare MACs in constant time (so that timing does not reveal how many characters matched), store the counter in non-volatile memory so a reboot does not reset it, and keep the key in protected storage (Chapter 15). With a shared key, any device holding the key can also forge messages; digital signatures, where only the sender holds the private key, remove that limitation and also provide non-repudiation.

Privacy by design

Security protects data from attackers; privacy protects people, including from the system’s own operators. Practical principles include:

  • Data minimization: collect only what the application needs, at the coarsest resolution that works.
  • Local processing: compute on the device or at the edge, and send results instead of raw data (Chapter 18).
  • Purpose limitation and retention limits: use data only for the stated purpose, and delete it when it is no longer needed.
  • Transparency and control: tell people what is collected, and let them see, export and delete it.
  • Separation of identity: decouple device identifiers and data from the owner’s identity where possible, while still allowing the owner to prove ownership.

Many of these principles are also legal requirements (Chapter 17).

14.3 In depth (optional)

Optional, in-depth explanation. This section goes beyond the rest of the chapter into the math and physics behind it. Nothing later in the book depends on it; skip it on a first reading.

How strong is strong enough?

Brute force and key length. A secret chosen uniformly from \(2^k\) possibilities takes, on average, \(2^{k-1}\) guesses to find. An attacker testing \(10^{12}\) keys per second needs about \(2^{55}/10^{12} \approx 10\) hours for a 56-bit key and \(2^{127}/10^{12} \approx 5\times10^{18}\) years for a 128-bit key. The difference is not 2.3 times but \(2^{72}\) times. Hence 128 bits as the modern floor for symmetric keys.

Human-chosen secrets are much weaker. A 6-digit PIN has \(10^6 \approx 2^{20}\) values; an 8-character password of lowercase letters \(26^8 \approx 2^{37.6}\). Default passwords shared by a whole product line have, effectively, zero bits. Rate limiting and lockout help online, but not against an attacker who has captured a hash and guesses offline.

Truncated authentication tags. A message authentication code (such as HMAC-SHA-256) is often truncated to save bytes on constrained links. An attacker who forges a message without the key succeeds with probability \(2^{-t}\) per attempt for a \(t\)-bit tag. A 32-bit tag gives one success in about 4 billion attempts: acceptable on a LoRa link limited to a few hundred messages per hour, unacceptable on a fast network that allows millions of attempts.

Nonces and the birthday bound. Many encryption modes need a nonce that never repeats under the same key. If nonces are chosen randomly from \(2^b\) values, the probability that \(n\) messages contain at least one repeat is approximately

\[ P_\text{collision} \approx 1 - e^{-n^2/2^{b+1}} \approx \frac{n^2}{2^{b+1}} . \]

With 96-bit random nonces (the AES-GCM standard size), \(n = 2^{32}\) messages give \(P \approx 2^{-33}\): safe. With a 32-bit random nonce, collisions become likely after about \(2^{16} = 65\,536\) messages, a few days of a sensor reporting every few seconds. A counter avoids collisions entirely, provided it survives reboots (see Activity 8’s extension).

Risk as likelihood times impact. Threat modeling often scores each threat as

\[ \text{risk} = \text{likelihood} \times \text{impact}, \]

on simple 1–5 scales. The product is crude, but it forces two separate judgments and makes the ranking discussable. A cheap attack with small impact (reading one room’s temperature) can rank above an expensive attack with large impact (extracting a key from silicon), and that ranking is a design decision, not a fact.

Exercise. A command channel uses HMAC tags truncated to 24 bits and accepts up to 10 commands per second. How long would an attacker need, on average, to get one forged command accepted? What would you change?

14.4 Design trade-offs

Decision Considerations
Shared keys vs. public-key cryptography Shared keys are fast and small but any key holder can impersonate others; public keys cost more computation but scale and give non-repudiation
Hop-by-hop vs. end-to-end security Hop-by-hop (TLS to a broker) is simple but exposes data at each intermediary; end-to-end protects data through brokers and proxies
Security vs. availability Locking out a device after failed checks can itself become a denial-of-service vector
Data collection vs. privacy More data enables more features and more harm

14.5 Common pitfalls

  • Default or shared passwords across all units.
  • Disabling certificate verification to “make TLS work”.
  • Rolling your own cryptography or using outdated algorithms (MD5, SHA-1, DES, RC4).
  • No replay protection on commands.
  • Secrets in source code or firmware images, readable by anyone with the binary.
  • Debug interfaces left enabled in production (serial consoles, JTAG, open telnet).
  • Logging sensitive data in plain text.
  • Treating privacy as a policy document rather than as design decisions about what data exists.
NoteAdvanced track

Red-team review. Exchange your project’s architecture and repository with another team. Perform a structured security code review: threat-model their system with STRIDE, review their code for the pitfalls above, and write a short report with findings ranked by severity and concrete fixes. Chapter 22 describes the method in detail.

Lightweight cryptography. In 2023 NIST selected the Ascon family as its standard for lightweight cryptography, aimed at devices too constrained for AES-GCM. Read the NIST announcement and compare Ascon’s code size and performance with AES-GCM on a microcontroller.

Post-quantum readiness. Devices deployed today may still be in service when large quantum computers can break ECC and RSA. NIST published its first post-quantum standards (ML-KEM and ML-DSA) in 2024. What would it take to support them on an ESP32-class device, and why does this matter most for firmware-signing keys?
